All hazards

Refinery disaster and fuel shortage

All five of Utah's oil refineries sit along one short stretch of I-15, from Rose Park in Salt Lake City to Bountiful. Utah OED (opens source in a new tab) In a worst case, if a major earthquake wrecked them all at once, people nearby could face fire and smoke, and Utah could run short of gasoline and diesel. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab) U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab) Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab)

Make my plan

This is a worst case, not a prediction. But a 2019 planning report produced with Utah's energy office names a major earthquake as the greatest threat to the state's fuel supply. It says a magnitude 7.0 earthquake on the Salt Lake City segment of the Wasatch fault would likely affect oil and fuel pipelines, production, and delivery. Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab) The FEMA-funded scenario for that earthquake warns of ruptured tanks, fire that could spread through a refinery, and long shutdowns. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab) This guide has two parts: staying safe near the refineries, and getting through a long fuel shortage anywhere in Utah.

Why plan for losing all five refineries at once

  • Utah has five oil refineries, all along I-15 between Rose Park in Salt Lake City and Bountiful. Together they can process about 210,000 barrels of crude oil a day, almost one-third of the Rocky Mountain region's refining capacity. Utah OED (opens source in a new tab) EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • The wind map farther down this page numbers the five refineries from north to south. They are (1) HF Sinclair's Woods Cross Refinery, which is in West Bountiful; (2) Silver Eagle in Woods Cross; (3) Big West Oil in North Salt Lake; (4) Chevron's Salt Lake Refinery; and (5) Marathon's Salt Lake City refinery. The federal government's refinery list shows Marathon's refinery under the company name Tesoro. EIA (opens source in a new tab) HF Sinclair (opens source in a new tab) Marathon Petroleum (opens source in a new tab)
  • The FEMA-funded scenario for a magnitude 7.0 earthquake says several of the refineries are next to the Wasatch fault. It says the greatest risk is usually to the big round storage tanks, which can rupture and spill, and that fire could spread through a refinery. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • Davis County's hazard plan says ground movement and liquefaction could severely damage the refineries. Liquefaction is when wet, sandy soil turns soft like quicksand during shaking. The plan also says many petroleum pipelines cross the fault or run within half a mile of it. Davis County (opens source in a new tab) UGS (opens source in a new tab)
  • The same earthquake would hit the pipelines too: the scenario assumes that almost all buried lines will break where they cross the fault. EERI Utah scenario (opens source in a new tab)
  • Even undamaged refineries could be shut for several weeks or longer while they wait for crude oil, according to the 2019 planning report for Utah's energy office. Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's health department wrote its February 2026 earthquake plan around a magnitude 6.5 earthquake on the same fault segment. The plan expects that, for as long as the emergency response lasts, there will be only limited sources inside Utah for new supplies of fuel. It also expects power outages that may last 30 days or longer and natural gas outages for weeks. Utah DHHS (opens source in a new tab)
  • A refinery here has exploded before. In November 2009, a hydrogen explosion at the Silver Eagle refinery in Woods Cross damaged more than 100 homes. CSB (opens source in a new tab)
  • How likely is the earthquake? Utah's hazard plan puts the chance of a magnitude 6.75 or larger earthquake between 2014 and 2063 at 5.8% on the Salt Lake City segment and 18% somewhere on the Wasatch fault zone. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)

If you live, work, or go to school near the refineries

  • Sign up for your county's emergency alerts now. Davis County sends them by text, phone, or email. Our Alerts page has the sign-up for every county, including Salt Lake County. Davis County (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't count on a single warning. During a January 2009 refinery fire in Woods Cross, some automated evacuation calls came late, and some went to people outside the evacuation zone. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • The fire department at the scene decides what people nearby should do. Leaving isn't always the safest choice. Salt Lake County's plan for hazardous-materials emergencies says sheltering in place (staying indoors) may be better. It depends on the size, type, and location of the chemical release. Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab)
  • Both have happened here. In January 2009, residents within half a mile of the Silver Eagle refinery were asked to evacuate. In April 2016, during an asphalt fire at the Foreland plant in Woods Cross, the fire department told people to stay indoors and close their windows. KSL (opens source in a new tab) FOX 13 News Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're told to evacuate, go right away. Davis County Health (opens source in a new tab) Follow the exact route officials give you. A shortcut can put you in the path of danger. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) For where to go and how to find a shelter, the Stay or go guide has more.
  • You can shelter in place before any order comes. Take the steps below if you see a lot of smoke or dust in the air, or if your eyes, nose, or throat start to burn. Then listen for official instructions. Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • After an earthquake, look at your building before you shelter in it. Our Earthquake page lists what to check. If it's damaged, or you smell natural gas inside, go outside, move away from it, and don't go back in. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Enbridge Gas (opens source in a new tab) Then shelter in the closest building that isn't damaged. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're told to shelter in place: bring everyone and your pets inside. Close and lock outside doors and windows. Close vents and the fireplace damper (the flap that shuts the chimney). Turn off fans, air conditioning, and forced-air heat (the furnace). Davis County Health (opens source in a new tab) Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Never close the damper on a fire or warm ashes. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Shelter in a room above ground, not the basement, with as few windows and doors as possible. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Why not the basement? Many toxic gases are heavier than air and tend to settle in low places. FEMA (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're told to seal the room, use thick plastic sheeting and duct tape over windows, doors, and air vents. Cut the sheeting to size ahead of time. A sealed room is a temporary measure, not a place to stay for long. Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Before you seal it, turn off any propane or kerosene heater and put out candles. In a closed room, poisonous carbon monoxide gas can build up quickly. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • An explosion can shatter windows in nearby homes, as the November 2009 Silver Eagle explosion did. CSB (opens source in a new tab) If you're told there's a danger of explosion, close the shades, blinds, or curtains and stay away from the windows. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • If an explosion makes things fall around you, get under a sturdy table or desk. If it's safe, leave the area quickly and don't stop for belongings. Always follow the instructions of local officials. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're caught outside, move away right away. Stay upwind: the wind should blow from you toward the fire or leak. Stay uphill. If you're already in the smoke or fumes, move crosswind to get out. Walk so the wind hits your left or right side, not your face or back. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) FEMA (opens source in a new tab) Then go into the closest building that isn't damaged. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • If a fire or chemical leak starts while you're driving, drive away from it. Then stop and go into the closest building that isn't damaged. Davis County Health (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) If you have to stay in the car, keep the windows and vents closed and the heater and air conditioner off. Davis County Health (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) If you're driving out because you were told to evacuate, follow the route officials give you. Keep the windows and vents closed and the heater and air conditioner off on the way. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Schools have their own plans. Under the Standard Response Protocol used in Utah schools, the action for a hazardous-materials emergency is to seal the room. Canyons School District (opens source in a new tab) Don't call or go to your child's school during the emergency. Davis School District says phone lines and roads must stay clear for responders. Afterward, students are released only to parents or listed emergency contacts who show photo ID. Davis School District (opens source in a new tab) Salt Lake City School District asks parents to wait for official word before driving to a school. Salt Lake City School District (opens source in a new tab)
  • Call 911 for an emergency, not for information. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Flames and smoke at a refinery are not always an emergency. Utah's environmental agency calls flaring a regular safety procedure. The Salt Lake Tribune (opens source in a new tab) In September 2024, local refineries flared and thick smoke could be seen across the valley. The fire department called it normal flare-off operations. KSL (opens source in a new tab) If smoke is reaching you, bring everyone inside and close the windows while you check for official news and instructions. Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab)
  • Wait until officials say it is safe (the all-clear). Until then, stay in your shelter room with a battery-powered or hand-crank radio on, unless you're told to evacuate. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) After a 2019 tank fire in Deer Park, Texas, a second shelter-in-place order came the day after the fire was out, because benzene levels in the air rose. U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab)
  • Want to know the plan? Each county's Local Emergency Planning Committee writes the chemical emergency plan. Salt Lake County's meetings are open to anyone. EPA (opens source in a new tab) Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)

Where the smoke would go: wind history

Where smoke goes depends on the wind at that hour, and smoke levels in towns are hard to predict. EPA (opens source in a new tab) But the wind here has habits. We counted ten years of hourly wind readings at two weather stations: the Salt Lake City airport and Legacy Parkway in Davis County. Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) Pick a season and a time of day to see which way the wind blew most often.

  • Night and early morning: the wind tends to blow down the valley, from the south toward the Great Salt Lake. Univ. of Utah study (opens source in a new tab) Utah's air quality agency says a lake breeze brings that air back the next day. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) By our count, between midnight and 6 a.m. the airport wind blew toward the northwest or north on about 59% of hours. Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) Census Bureau (opens source in a new tab) On Legacy Parkway, the wind was calm or shifting on about 20% of those hours and blew toward the north or northwest on about 35%. From the three Davis County refineries, that is toward the lake and the west side of south Davis County. Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) Census Bureau (opens source in a new tab)
  • Afternoon: the pattern flips. As the valley heats up, the wind turns to blow from the northwest toward the southeast. Univ. of Utah study (opens source in a new tab) A study of airport records from 1948 to 2003 found that shift on more than 70% of dry days from April through October. Univ. of Utah study (opens source in a new tab) By our count, between noon and 6 p.m. the wind blew toward the southeast or south on about 50% of hours at the airport and 53% on Legacy Parkway. Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) Census Bureau (opens source in a new tab)
  • Morning and evening are changeover times. A study of fair-weather summer days in Wasatch Front and other Intermountain West valleys found that wind direction is steadiest at night and least steady during the changeovers. Wind study (opens source in a new tab)
  • Everyday evidence: at the state's air monitor in Bountiful, pollution that researchers linked to refineries and industry showed up mainly when the wind blew from the south-southwest, the direction of the refineries. Bountiful air study (opens source in a new tab)
  • Winter inversions: the mountains around the valley form a bowl that traps cold air, and pollution from industry and other sources gets trapped near the ground with it. A typical Utah winter has about five or six inversions that last several days, and it often takes a strong storm to clear one. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) The wind is very light during these inversions. NWS (opens source in a new tab) By our count, on winter nights (December through February, midnight to 6 a.m.), the wind on Legacy Parkway was calm or shifting on about 25% of hours. Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) An inversion acts like a lid, and with little wind, smoke can build up in a valley at night. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Storm days break the pattern. Strong winds from the south usually come ahead of a storm. They would push smoke north. Strong winds from the northwest often follow a cold front. They would push smoke southeast. NWS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Strong windstorms from the east are rare but violent. They blow down the mountain slopes and hit the northern Wasatch Front every few years on average. NWS (opens source in a new tab) In September 2020, one brought gusts of 99 mph on U.S. 89 at Park Lane in Farmington and 75 mph in Centerville. NOAA (opens source in a new tab) A wind from the east would push smoke west. On the way, it would cross Rose Park and parts of Woods Cross and North Salt Lake. Census Bureau (opens source in a new tab) The airport sits too far from the mountains to feel most of these storms, so its numbers on the map show fewer of them. Univ. of Utah study (opens source in a new tab)
  • The wind can swing within an hour or two. On April 26, 2016, asphalt caught fire at the Foreland plant in Woods Cross, which is not one of the five refineries. KSL (opens source in a new tab) EIA (opens source in a new tab) Around noon, the airport had a light breeze from the southeast. Then the wind went calm. By 2 p.m., it blew from the northwest at about 17 mph (15 knots). Iowa Mesonet (opens source in a new tab) The fire chief said the wind carried the smoke and smell in every direction. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Wind decides who is exposed. After a 2018 refinery explosion in Superior, Wisconsin, officials used a forecast of where smoke or fumes would drift to draw the evacuation zone. It reached 2 miles north of the refinery but 10 miles south, the way the wind was blowing. The zone was also a precaution in case a highly toxic chemical stored at the refinery was released, which never happened. U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab)
  • Smoke can travel high above the ground. The heat of a big fire drives smoke high into the air, where it stays until it cools and comes back down. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • In a real emergency, officials have better tools than averages. For a large chemical release, an emergency manager can call the National Weather Service. Forecasters can then give hourly readings, a wind forecast for that exact spot, and a computer forecast of where the smoke or gas will go. NWS (opens source in a new tab) Salt Lake County's plan for hazardous-materials emergencies tells a company reporting a release to also contact the Weather Service's Salt Lake office whenever weather may be a factor. Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Use this map to plan. When it's real, do what the official alert says.
  • Check the wind yourself: the Weather Service's hourly forecast graph shows wind speed and direction for the next two days. Utah's air quality site shows an hourly wind reading for Davis County and for Rose Park. Smoke and fumes: protect your health

  • Refinery fire smoke is soot mixed with gases. U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab) After a 2025 refinery fire in Martinez, California, the county health department said the smoke included benzene, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, and xylene. KTVU FOX 2 (opens source in a new tab)
  • It can make many people sick at once. After a 2012 refinery fire in Richmond, California, about 15,000 people sought medical treatment in the following weeks for problems such as trouble breathing, chest pain, sore throat, and headaches. About 20 were admitted to a hospital. U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab)
  • Children, older adults, and people with long-term conditions like asthma are especially sensitive to air pollution. Utah DHHS (opens source in a new tab) Even a short exposure to sulfur dioxide can make breathing hard. People with asthma, especially children, are sensitive to this effect. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't use your nose as the alarm. Hydrogen sulfide smells like rotten eggs, but at high levels people lose the ability to smell it within minutes. ATSDR (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you're told to evacuate, go right away. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) If not, and smoke reaches you, stay inside with windows and doors closed. EPA (opens source in a new tab) If you're told to shelter in place, also turn off fans, air conditioning, and forced-air heat, unless the alert says something different. Salt Lake County (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) If there's no order to stay inside and your home gets too hot to keep closed, find shelter somewhere else. EPA (opens source in a new tab) The Extreme heat page lists heat illness warning signs.
  • An N95 mask does not protect against gases. NIOSH (opens source in a new tab) Masks like it, called respirators, can also make breathing harder, so people with heart or lung problems and pregnant people should ask a doctor before using one. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • No air cleaner removes every pollutant. To filter gases, a portable air cleaner needs an activated carbon filter with a large amount of filter material, or another filter made for gases. The rating on most air cleaners, the clean air delivery rate (CADR), covers particles only. No widely used rating covers gases. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Call 911 or go to an emergency department right away for severe trouble breathing or chest pain. CDC (opens source in a new tab) If you've been told to shelter in place, call 911 instead of going outside. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) For questions about a chemical exposure, Utah Poison Control is free and open 24 hours a day: 1-800-222-1222. CDC (opens source in a new tab) Utah Poison Control Center (opens source in a new tab)
  • If a chemical gets on your skin or clothes, take off your clothing and seal it in a plastic bag. Wash with soap and water, rinse your eyes, and call Poison Control or get medical care. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) CDC (opens source in a new tab) Don't eat or drink anything that may be contaminated. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) The Chemical spills & hazmat page has more steps and kit items.
  • Know what the air quality apps can't tell you. Utah's hourly county updates show fine particles (soot) and ozone, not gases like benzene or hydrogen sulfide. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) A state study uses a mobile lab to measure hazardous air pollutants in neighborhoods including North Salt Lake, Bountiful, and Woods Cross. It is suspended until further notice. The plan was to report every six months through December 2026. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • Oil and fuel can reach creeks. In a 2010 pipeline spill, crude oil ran down Red Butte Creek to Liberty Park, and a thin layer of oil (a sheen) reached the Jordan River. Officials told people and pets to stay away from the water. Utah Dept. of Health (opens source in a new tab) KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Boiling or disinfecting can't make water safe if it contains fuel or toxic chemicals. Use bottled water or another source, and ask your health department what to do. CDC (opens source in a new tab)

What could happen to fuel across Utah

  • Utah's refineries make more fuel than Utah uses, and four major pipelines tie the state to its neighbors. Two carry fuel out: UNEV, to southern Utah and Las Vegas, and a line to Idaho and the Pacific Northwest. Two bring fuel in: Pioneer, from Wyoming into northern Utah, and a line from the Gulf Coast into southeastern Utah. Utah OED (opens source in a new tab) Enterprise Products (opens source in a new tab)
  • Northern Utah: the Pioneer pipeline from Wyoming moves about 60,000 barrels of fuel a day. KPCW (opens source in a new tab) In 2024 Utah used about 31 million barrels of gasoline and 15 million barrels of diesel and similar fuel. That is about 128,000 barrels a day by our arithmetic, roughly twice what the Pioneer line carries. EIA (opens source in a new tab) EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • St. George, Cedar City, and southwestern Utah: your pipeline starts at the refineries. UNEV carries fuel from Woods Cross to a terminal near Cedar City and on to Las Vegas. The Pioneer pipeline from Wyoming ends at a terminal in North Salt Lake and can also deliver fuel into UNEV. HF Sinclair (opens source in a new tab) Las Vegas also gets fuel by pipeline from Southern California. EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Moab, Price, and eastern Utah: the Gulf Coast line belongs to a pipeline company, Enterprise Products. It has a truck terminal in Grand County with direct access to I-70. Utah OED (opens source in a new tab) Enterprise Products (opens source in a new tab) Enterprise says the terminal serves the areas around Moab and Grand Junction, Colorado, and can reach markets in eastern Utah and western Colorado. The terminal can load up to 20,000 barrels a day into trucks. Enterprise Products (opens source in a new tab)
  • Logan and towns near the Idaho border: driving into Idaho for fuel may not help. The pipeline that supplies southern Idaho's fuel terminals comes from Utah's refineries. EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • The state says there is limited infrastructure to bring in refined fuel from farther east. Utah OED (opens source in a new tab) The Rocky Mountain region is largely cut off from the country's big refining centers, and shifting fuel to isolated markets can take considerable time. EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Stored fuel is limited. A 2019 planning report for the state said the refineries kept, on average, about as much refined fuel as they made in six days. It was stored in above-ground tanks at the refineries. The report warned that after a magnitude 7.0 earthquake this limited stock might not be reachable. It did not say how many days Utah's fuel would last. Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab)
  • A smaller example: Colorado's only refinery shut down in late December 2022. By February 20, 2023, average gasoline prices had risen 51% in Colorado and 27% across the Rocky Mountain region. Prices rose 9% nationwide in the same weeks. EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • How long a refinery stays down depends on the damage. Refineries shut down as a precaution for a hurricane can often restart within a few days if nothing major is damaged. EIA (opens source in a new tab) After a 2018 explosion and fire, a Wisconsin refinery was rebuilt with a restart planned for 2023, about five years later. U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab) After a fire and explosions in 2019, the owner of a Philadelphia refinery announced within days that the refinery would shut down. The owner had come out of bankruptcy less than a year earlier. A court approved the sale of the refinery, and by 2022 the buyer was tearing it down. U.S. Chemical Safety Board (opens source in a new tab)
  • Diesel matters as much as gasoline. Diesel trucks, trains, and barges move nearly everything people buy, and public buses and school buses commonly run on diesel. EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • FEMA's planning guides list a lack of fuel for freight among the problems in moving food after a disaster, and say government and nonprofits can't replace commercial food supply. FEMA (opens source in a new tab)
  • The refineries also make jet fuel. EIA (opens source in a new tab) Chevron says its Salt Lake refinery is an important supplier of jet fuel for Salt Lake City International Airport. Chevron (opens source in a new tab)

What the state can do, and what it means for you

  • Utah's governor can declare an energy emergency when a severe fuel shortage threatens essential services, transportation, or the economy. It lasts up to 60 days unless the Legislature extends it. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • During an energy emergency, the governor can order all suppliers and users to supply or use less fuel. The governor can also decide how fuel is divided, and when and how it is sold. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • Expect possible limits at the pump. A 2019 planning report prepared with Utah's energy office lists steps the state could order if the governor declares a state of emergency. Gas stations could open only on certain days and sell fewer gallons per sale. Sales could depend on whether your license plate number is odd or even. Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab) Utah's earthquake handbook also says gasoline may be in short supply and rationing (limits on how much you can buy) may be needed. Utah Seismic Safety Commission (opens source in a new tab)
  • Emergency services come first. The same 2019 report says Utah's plan for energy emergencies includes a fuel program for emergency responders. The governor can start the program by executive order. Fuel from the program's storage may be used only for emergency or critical services. The report describes no priority for households. Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah has studied a state fuel reserve. We searched and found no law or program that creates one. A 2024 law ordered the study, the energy office reported it complete in May 2025, and in 2026 the Legislature repealed the study law as completed. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab) Utah OED (opens source in a new tab) Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • After a disaster damages refineries or pipelines, EPA can temporarily waive some fuel rules in the affected area. That lets more kinds of fuel be sold there. A waiver can last up to 20 days, and EPA can renew it. EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • The country's emergency oil reserve holds crude oil, not gasoline. DOE (opens source in a new tab) Crude oil has to go through a refinery before it can fuel a car. EIA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's price-gouging law bans excessive prices only after the President declares an emergency or major disaster, or the governor declares a state of emergency under Utah's disaster law. An energy emergency alone is not enough. The law applies while the governor's state of emergency lasts, or for 30 days after a presidential emergency begins. It covers only the area the disaster directly affects, not other places, even if prices rise there. An excessive price generally means more than 10% above the seller's highest price in the 30 days before the declaration. Sellers whose own costs rose can charge more. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab) Report gouging to the Utah Division of Consumer Protection. Utah Division of Consumer Protection (opens source in a new tab)

Insurance: who pays for the damage

  • Nearly every homeowners policy covers fire, smoke, and explosions, the Utah Insurance Department says. Utah Insurance Department (opens source in a new tab)
  • A standard policy doesn't cover earthquake shaking, but it generally covers a fire that follows an earthquake. Triple-I (opens source in a new tab)
  • Ask your agent about refinery smoke. One common standard policy form leaves out smoke from industrial operations, so don't assume soot damage from a refinery fire is covered. ISO HO-3 sample (opens source in a new tab)
  • After the 2009 Silver Eagle explosion, neighbors filed more than 270 damage claims. KSL (opens source in a new tab) Some residents had to move out while their homes were repaired. CSB (opens source in a new tab) Adjusters (people who inspect damage for insurance claims) came from both the homeowners' insurance companies and the refinery. KSL (opens source in a new tab) Utah Insurance Department (opens source in a new tab)
  • If your home is damaged: cover broken windows and keep your receipts. Don't make permanent repairs until your insurance company has inspected and you have agreed with the company on the cost. Utah Insurance Department (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you choose to leave without an official evacuation order, the Utah Insurance Department says you pay your own costs for being away from home. Even with an order, policies usually pay only when officials keep you out of your home because of direct physical damage to nearby property. Rules differ between insurance companies. Save the order and every receipt. Utah Insurance Department (opens source in a new tab) These are rules about money, not safety. After an earthquake, if your building is damaged, go outside and move away from it. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab) The Insurance guide covers earthquake and renters coverage.

Before: what to do now

  • Make it a habit now, before any shortage, to keep your gas tank at least half full. Be Ready Utah says that in emergencies, gas pumps often don't work or quickly run out. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov goes further and says to keep the tank full. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep walking shoes in the car, in case you have to walk. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Store only a little fuel, and store it safely. Utah's fire code allows up to 10 gallons in total of gasoline and similar fuels for equipment, in approved containers, in a private garage. More than that needs a storage cabinet made for flammable liquids. 2024 IFC (Utah) (opens source in a new tab) But Be Ready Utah says not to store fuel in your home or in a garage attached to it. A separate building, like a shed, is best. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Lehi's fire department recommends no more than 5 gallons of gasoline at home. Ask your own fire department about local limits. Lehi Fire (opens source in a new tab) The Fuel section of the 90 days without utilities guide has more.
  • Never keep gas cans in a house or basement. Never keep them near anything that could light the fumes. That includes a stove, a heater, and a pilot light (the small flame that stays lit in some water heaters and furnaces). CPSC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Use only child-resistant containers made for fuel. Any other container, like a jug or a plastic bag, is extremely dangerous. When you buy a gas can, new or used, make sure it has a flame arrestor. This simple device blocks burning fumes and helps keep the can from bursting. CPSC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Plan how you'd get to work, school, and the store with little or no gas: carpools, transit, biking, working from home, and combining errands into one trip. UDOT TravelWise (opens source in a new tab)
  • Know transit's limits. FrontRunner trains run on diesel. UTA (opens source in a new tab) About 8% of UTA's buses were electric as of December 2024. UTA (opens source in a new tab) Some of its other buses run on natural gas. UTA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep a supply of the medicines you take every day. An emergency can make it hard to get a refill or find an open pharmacy. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Build up your food storage. Stores are restocked by truck, and trucks run on diesel. EIA (opens source in a new tab) The Food storage guide shows how.
  • If you're choosing a car, a plug-in hybrid can run on electricity or on gasoline. The Department of Energy says hybrid and electric vehicles add flexibility when fuel supplies are disrupted. DOE (opens source in a new tab)

During a shortage: make fuel last

  • Once a shortage starts, don't panic-buy, and don't top off a tank that still has plenty of gas. The state's 2019 planning report says that in a short-term interruption of fuel supply, Utah could ask the public to take voluntary steps, including not topping off fuel tanks. Utah OED and NASEO (opens source in a new tab) In the 2021 Colonial Pipeline shutdown, many gas stations ran out of gasoline. The Energy Information Administration said this happened both because the pipeline was down and because people bought more gasoline once stations started running out. EIA (opens source in a new tab) GasBuddy warned then that panic buying and hoarding make outages longer and much worse. GasBuddy (opens source in a new tab)
  • Never put gasoline in anything except a vehicle or an approved fuel container. During the 2021 shortage, the Consumer Product Safety Commission warned people not to fill plastic bags with gasoline. CBS News (opens source in a new tab)
  • Set a gas can on the ground to fill it. Never fill one inside a car, a trunk, or a pickup bed. Unified Fire Authority (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't waste gas driving around looking for gas. Use an app or call the station to check first. NPR (opens source in a new tab)
  • A rush of buyers can empty a station faster than usual, and independent stations may be resupplied after the big brands. FEMA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Drive gently. Speeding, hard acceleration, and hard braking can lower gas mileage by roughly 15% to 30% on the highway and 10% to 40% in stop-and-go traffic. FuelEconomy.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • Group your errands into one trip instead of going home between stops, and share rides. UDOT TravelWise (opens source in a new tab)
  • Follow the fuel rules the state announces. During an energy emergency the governor can set when and how fuel is sold. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • Schools may close. Utah districts and charter schools that close for an emergency can ask the state to waive the 180-school-day requirement. Utah Board of Education (opens source in a new tab)
  • Out of a medicine and can't reach your doctor quickly? A Utah pharmacist may give one emergency refill of a medicine you already take, but only as much as the emergency requires. The law limits when that can be more than a 3-day supply. This rule doesn't cover controlled substances; ask your pharmacist whether any of your medicines are controlled. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab) The Health and medicine section of the 90 days without utilities guide has more.
  • Watch for scams. FEMA doesn't charge application fees, so anyone who wants money to help you get FEMA aid is running a scam. FTC (opens source in a new tab)
