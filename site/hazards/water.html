Water & sewer outages

Water mains break, supplies get contaminated, and a big earthquake could cut water and sewer for weeks. Stored water and knowing what a boil order means go a long way.

Add this to my plan

Utah has 978 public water systems, and when one becomes dangerously contaminated it issues a boil order. After a major Salt Lake County earthquake, FEMA modeling predicts more than 300,000 households without drinkable water for 90 days. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) Deseret News (opens source in a new tab)

Why it matters in Utah

  • When drinking water becomes dangerously contaminated, the local water system issues a boil order, and it has 24 hours to notify people who could drink the water. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) EPA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Boil orders happen somewhere in Utah every year. In 2026, Riverton (irrigation water in the lines), Junction (burn-scar runoff), and Beaver (a broken supply line) all had one. KUTV (opens source in a new tab) Utah Public Notice (opens source in a new tab) KUTV (opens source in a new tab)
  • FEMA modeling predicts that a magnitude 7.0 Salt Lake County earthquake could leave more than 300,000 households without drinkable water for 90 days. Deseret News (opens source in a new tab)
  • More than two million Wasatch Front residents rely on aging aqueducts; a break could leave hundreds of thousands without water for six months or longer. Utah Seismic Safety Commission (opens source in a new tab)
  • In a 2024 Envision Utah poll, Utahns said they worried more about losing drinking water, power, and sewer service after an earthquake than about deaths or long-term devastation. Salt Lake Tribune (opens source in a new tab)
  • Utah's water systems have also seen break-ins, cyberattacks, and accidents. See the record on this page.

Who's most affected

  • Families with babies: during a boil order, ready-to-use formula is the safest choice if you use formula. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Homes on private wells, which need disinfecting and testing after a flood. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Everyone after a big earthquake, when water, sewer, power, and gas could be out for days to months. UGS (opens source in a new tab)

What to do

1 Before

  • Store at least 1 gallon of water per person per day, starting with a 14-day supply. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • Preparing for an outage that lasts months? The 90 days without utilities guide covers storing and treating water at that scale.
  • Strap your water heater: the tank holds clean water you can use in an outage. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab) FEMA (opens source in a new tab)
  • Look up which water system serves your address, and know where it posts notices. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • Build an emergency toilet: two buckets, heavy-duty bags, and kitty litter. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep plain unscented bleach, hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol, moist towelettes, garbage bags, and ties. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)

2 During

  • Boil water advisory: bring water to a full rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 feet) and let it cool. Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, ice, pets, and baby formula. If your water system's notice says to boil longer, follow it. EPA (opens source in a new tab) CDC (opens source in a new tab) Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • Do-not-drink order: boiling won't help, because the problem may be chemicals. Use bottled water for drinking and cooking. Do-not-use orders are rare: use bottled water for everything. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Dishes: a dishwasher with a sanitizing cycle is generally fine. To hand-wash, soak dishes for 1 minute in 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per gallon of warm water. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Can't boil? Utah DEQ allows 1/8 teaspoon of plain household bleach per gallon; let it stand 30 minutes. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)
  • No running water? Your water heater, pipes, and ice cubes hold clean water. To use the heater, turn off its power or gas first, open the drain at the bottom, and open a hot-water faucet. Never use water from a heating radiator. FEMA (opens source in a new tab) CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • After an earthquake, don't flush or pour anything down drains until you know the sewer pipes are safe. Use a two-bucket toilet: one for urine, one lined with two bags and kitty litter. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Pool and spa water is fine for washing, but not for drinking. CDC (opens source in a new tab)

3 After

  • When a boil order lifts, flush your household pipes and throw out ice made during the order. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • Bury waste at least 200 feet from water and living areas, or keep it in a lidded can until it can be collected. Never dump it in streams or canals. Be Ready Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • Refill and date your stored water. Ready.gov (opens source in a new tab)

Has it happened here? Attacks and accidents involving Utah's water supplies

Yes to both, though not the way movies show it. We searched Utah news coverage and state and federal records through November 2026. For break-ins, sabotage, and poisoning, we also ran keyword searches of digitized Utah newspapers back to the 1850s. We found a bomb that failed to go off in 1917, tank break-ins, vandalism, cyberattacks, and many accidents. We did not find a confirmed case of anyone deliberately poisoning a Utah public water supply. A search like this can miss things, especially older events and ones that never made the news, so read these lists as examples, not a full count.

Break-ins and tampering

  • Ogden, 1917: a night watchman found a dynamite bomb, which had failed to go off, on the main pipe that fed the city's reservoirs. No arrests had been made when the newspaper reported it. Ogden Standard, 1917 (opens source in a new tab)
  • Salt Lake City, 1926: during a water shortage, investigators found that someone had tampered with the city's Big Cottonwood Conduit and turned canyon water toward farmland. Utah Humanities (opens source in a new tab)
  • Kaysville, 1994: vandals damaged a drinking water tank and disrupted water service to two neighborhoods. Davis County Clipper, 1994 (opens source in a new tab)
  • Park City, 2008: after a break-in at a water tank, crews told the 116 homes that got water from that tank to boil their water. Tests showed the water was safe to drink, and the order was lifted two days later. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Eagle Mountain, 2014: after a break-in at a storage tank, the city told residents not to use tap water at all, even for cooking, showering, or brushing teeth. KSL (opens source in a new tab) Tests found no contaminants, and the city of about 23,000 people went back to using the water the next night. AP (opens source in a new tab)
  • Santaquin, 2015: gunfire damaged equipment at one of the city's water tanks, and a city official said the city came close to leaving many people without water. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Mountain Green, 2019: after a break-in at a storage tank, customers were told to boil their drinking water. First test results the next day showed the water was safe. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Springville, Woodland Hills, and Tooele, 2020: three separate incidents at water tanks in three months led to a boil advisory in part of Springville, a boil order in Woodland Hills, and a do-not-drink order in part of Tooele. In all three, the water was found to be safe. KSL (opens source in a new tab) KUTV (opens source in a new tab) KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Federal law makes it a crime to tamper with a public water system, which the law defines as putting a contaminant into it, or otherwise interfering with how it works, with the intent to harm people. The penalty is up to 20 years in prison. U.S. Code (opens source in a new tab) A 2023 Utah law also makes it a felony to intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly destroy or seriously damage a water storage, treatment, or pumping facility in a way that causes widespread injury or damage. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)

Cyberattacks

  • In Utah, we did not find a cyberattack that contaminated drinking water, cut off water service, or led to a boil or do-not-drink notice.
  • A Utah city that state auditors did not name, June 2023: a cyberattack forced the water staff to run the system by hand for about three weeks. Recovery took 18 months and cost about $360,000. Water delivery was not affected. Utah Legislative Auditor (opens source in a new tab)
  • Summit County, 2021: malware (harmful software) hit a few office computers at Mountain Regional Water District. The county manager said it had no effect on operations or on the safety of water delivery or treatment. He also said the district lost a couple of years of office data, which was expected to be recovered. KPCW (opens source in a new tab)
  • Reported attempts, November 2025: the Deseret News reported in August 2026 that hackers had scouted Utah's water systems and tried to get in nearly 500 times in 46 minutes. The paper was citing ABC News, which had seen an intelligence note saying Utah public safety officials spotted the activity. State agencies did not answer the paper's requests for comment. Deseret News (opens source in a new tab) We found no report that any of the attempts succeeded or affected the water.
  • State auditors found in January 2026 that Utah's drinking water systems were not fully using basic cybersecurity practices. Fewer than a third of the systems that answered the auditors' survey had a plan for a cyber event, such as a cyberattack. The auditors also warned that the real risk may be higher than the number of reported attacks suggests. Utah Legislative Auditor (opens source in a new tab)
  • A 2026 Utah law requires every community water system to report a security breach to the state's Utah Cyber Center within two hours of discovering it. It also requires an emergency response plan, by December 31, 2026, for systems serving 3,300 or more people and by July 1, 2027, for smaller ones. Utah Code (opens source in a new tab)
  • Around the country: on July 30, 2026, the FBI and EPA said water and sewer utilities in at least seven states had reported cyber incidents that week, and that some of the activity disrupted how the systems ran. The alert did not name the states. FBI and EPA (opens source in a new tab)

Accidents that contaminated water

  • Sandy, February 2019: a pump malfunction released full-strength fluoride into part of the city's drinking water. A state analysis counted 239 cases of people with symptoms such as vomiting and headaches. KSL (opens source in a new tab) An independent review found failures by the city, the county health department, and the state. It said the public wasn't told to stop drinking the water until 10 days after the release. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Nibley, April 2015: diesel fuel got into a spring that feeds the city's drinking water system. Residents were told not to drink, cook with, or bathe in tap water, and that boiling would not help. KSL (opens source in a new tab) The do-not-drink order was lifted almost a week later. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Saratoga Springs, May 2010: residents got sick with campylobacter infections (a stomach illness caused by bacteria), and a boil order was in place for two days. The county health department counted 15 lab-confirmed cases at the time and could not confirm the source. KSL (opens source in a new tab) CDC's national report lists a 2010 campylobacter outbreak in a Utah community water system, with 628 cases. It does not name the city. It says a connection that let non-drinking water flow back into drinking water was a suspected or confirmed factor. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Syracuse and Clinton, June 2015: E. coli bacteria led to boil orders in both cities. Syracuse traced its problem to sprinkler and irrigation lines connected to drinking water lines. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • A Utah County city, 2023: 13 children got E. coli infections from untreated irrigation water, not from the drinking water system. Seven were hospitalized. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Stockton, 2014: a wildfire destroyed the town's water tank. Firefighting aircraft dropped fire retardant to protect a nearby house, and the retardant contaminated the water. Investigators believed the fire was set on purpose. KSL (opens source in a new tab) Nothing we read suggests the water system was the target.
  • Moroni, 2020: after a mechanical failure at one of the city's water sources, nitrate in the water rose. High levels of nitrate can be dangerous for babies. Residents were warned not to give the water to babies under six months or use it to make baby formula. FOX 13 News Utah (opens source in a new tab)
  • San Juan River, 2015: the Gold King Mine spill in Colorado sent polluted water down the river and through Utah, estimated to have passed between August 9 and 15. Utah Legislature (opens source in a new tab) On August 13, Utah environmental officials said the area's drinking water was safe. KUER (opens source in a new tab)
  • Tooele, 1983: 1,272 people got sick with diarrhea. CDC said the illness was possibly tied to a public water supply contaminated by flooding during Utah's spring thaw. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Provo River, 2015: a tanker truck crashed and spilled crude oil into the river. Utah agencies said they expected no effect on drinking water. KSL (opens source in a new tab)

Breaks, floods, fire, and drought

  • Huntsville, December 2024: a large leak drained the town's water supply, and residents were under a boil order until just before Christmas. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Torrey, June 2017: a break in the main water line left the town's system out of water, and a state of emergency was declared. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • South Jordan and West Jordan, July 2016: contractors hit a 42-inch water main, knocking out water to thousands. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Panguitch, July 2018: a flash flood ran over a burn scar (land burned by a wildfire) and contaminated the springs that supply the city's water. The city of 1,800 people was put under a "No Water Use" order. Intermountain Health (opens source in a new tab)
  • Beaver, Piute, and Sevier counties, July 2026: floods from burn scars disrupted drinking water and farm water systems serving Beaver, Marysvale, Junction, and Circleville, and damaged water infrastructure in Sevier County. The governor declared a state of emergency for all three counties. Utah Governor (opens source in a new tab)
  • Scofield and Echo, 2021: both communities ran out of drinking water during the drought. A tanker of drinking water was sent to Scofield, and water was trucked to Echo. Deseret News (opens source in a new tab) The same summer, Hyde Park told residents its tanks could run dry within days unless they cut water use in half. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • Magna earthquake, March 2020: after the magnitude 5.7 earthquake, the state Department of Environmental Quality reported a few water line breaks. It said that by that evening there were no interruptions in drinking water service. The Salt Lake Tribune (opens source in a new tab) A bigger earthquake would be different: FEMA modeling predicts that a magnitude 7.0 in Salt Lake County could leave more than 300,000 households without drinkable water for 90 days. Deseret News (opens source in a new tab)
  • Quail Creek Reservoir, 1989: nine miles from St. George, an earthen dike holding back the reservoir burst. The flood left about 100 farm families without irrigation water. Deseret News (opens source in a new tab)
  • In 2022, Utah's Division of Drinking Water responded to 50 drinking water emergencies across the state. KSL (opens source in a new tab)

What this record means for you

  • Do exactly what the notice says, and keep doing it until you are told the water is safe. A boil order, a do-not-drink order, and a do-not-use order are three different instructions, and boiling does not make water with chemicals in it safe. CDC (opens source in a new tab) Don't treat a notice as a false alarm because earlier ones ended with clean tests. An order stays in place until testing shows the water meets health standards. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • A notice should come fast, but it does not always. For the most serious problems, a water system must notify the people it serves as soon as it practically can, and no later than 24 hours after it learns of the problem. Utah rule R309-220 (opens source in a new tab) After the 2019 fluoride accident in Sandy, an independent review found that the public wasn't told to stop drinking the water until 10 days after the release. KSL (opens source in a new tab)
  • If something seems wrong with your water, call your water supplier first. If that doesn't settle it, your local health department also takes concerns from water customers. Utah DEQ, Division of Drinking Water (opens source in a new tab) Call 911 if anyone is in danger. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab) Find out now which system serves your address. Utah DEQ (opens source in a new tab)
  • If you see something suspicious at a water tank or plant, call 911 in an emergency. Otherwise, tell your local police who or what you saw, when, where, and why it seemed suspicious. DHS (opens source in a new tab)
  • Never connect secondary (pressurized irrigation) water to your home's drinking water lines. The state says a single connection like that can contaminate a whole water system, and asks you to call the water department if you see a neighbor digging on water or sprinkler lines. Utah DEQ, Division of Drinking Water (opens source in a new tab)
  • Don't drink untreated irrigation water or let children play in it. CDC (opens source in a new tab)
  • Keep stored water, so a notice is an inconvenience and not a crisis. Utah's Division of Emergency Management says to start with a 14-day supply. Utah DEM (opens source in a new tab)
  • On a private well? You are responsible for the safety of your own water. The state lab says to test it at least once a year. Utah Public Health Laboratory (opens source in a new tab)
